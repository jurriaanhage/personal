Summary

Rarely did I so doubt the correct title of a record. The front cover indicates that this is An Ocean In A Drop by A Drop In The Ocean, but the side and the cd itself mention also the name of Sergio Benchimol. I stuck with that name also because the CDDB database indicated that Benchimol is in fact the artist. He is not alone though; a host of artists accompanies him on his musical journey.

The music

Terral is the opener which seems at first rather lightfooted. There are resemblances to some of the chamber orchestra bands, but also plenty of Southamerican flair here, comparable to say Raimundo Rodulfo. The flute at first gives the music a bit of a Tull feel, but later use is way too subtle. The violin gives a bit of the air of After Crying now. In this way we have already heard violin and flute beside all the usual instruments, while the acoustic guitar in fact has the leading role.

The acoustic guitar also figures strongly on Janelas Do Ceu. Again it is accompanied by flute and I guess the violoncello. A rather somber piece this by comparison, repetitive, with a certain up and down movement to it. Halfway, we get a change of character as the flute sets in (think Paul Horn). The flute does sound a bit thin. The music remains classically inspired. Memorias De Viajens is no different, although now we are also treated to some sitar. If you look at the number of instruments Benchimol plays, you might think of Oldfield, but as it turns out, the music is quite different. The pace comes in again, led by a melodious flute and up-beat rhythms, like hand clapping. Quite danceable this. And the theme is a memorable one. The ending has a folky feel: rather simple acoustic guitar and flute.

Acoustic guitar again on Bamidbar, lined by low violin (or is this again the violoncello). Besides some rather overtly melodious parts, the music does have the tendency to sound somewhat vague. It happens, but does it lead anywhere. Maybe I am simply too impatient. A Spanish influence sets in halfway, with the acoustic guitar played lower and more pronounced. It will not burst loose (as I was hoping it would). Toca Da Cigarra is of the more melodious kind, supported on a sad sounding flute melody.

It will not surprise you much how Bossa Rio Jazz Waltz sounds. Again the flute plays the leading role, this time on a rather merry tune. Jardim Das Delicias is the longest track with slightly over eight minutes. It opens with light piano, taken over by sitar and if I am not mistaken accompanied by sax (in the other ear). There are vocals here, but they are very much buried under the slow music. The melodies are quite stately. Halfway do flute, piano and light drums and percussion enter the picture again, taking over the sitar part. Benchimol takes his time developing the ideas, which contrasts with the bloc of progressive artists that tries to fill up their tracks with ideas.

Maracatu is a bit more up-beat and such an infusion is surely welcome. The flute and sax work together to build an ethnic atmosphere, the percussion certainly helps there. The piano also adds to the percussion. There are some links to Cuneiform material here, also because the music tends to sound like a chamber orchestra in places. Moria continues the line upwards. The music becomes more ensemblelike, more tension rich too. This time the sax and violoncello that conspire, and the repetitive melody is indeed one that instills urgency. Think Kerman's 5UU's here, although the production is not as clean.

The Hunt is one of the more rocky tunes. For some reason though I continually reminded of ELP, although the guitar is very much like psychedelic rock. The drums are really straightforward, while the piano takes the place of the bass. A nice touch. Falling Times has a familiar theme, mainly because it is one that I remembered from earlier listening sessions with this one. Something rumbles very low here below the sax and the piano. It strikes me that the flute oriented tunes are much more lightfooted. The music around this are much starker, which for my tastes is a good thing. The vocals are not plainly audible, at least I can not hear what is being sung, but it does give the song a sinister feel. Afterwards the music is lightly up-beat with meandering sax.

On Exodus, the sax is plaintive, and this is indeed a sad, but beautiful lament. The title track sounds like seventies prog, with the keys sounding quite dated. The brassiness gives the impression of ELP, also because it is very Peter Gunnish when it comes to the melody. A bit of a muddle this one. The tune like character continues on the light footed A Rocha & O Mar. The sax is now more in easy listening mode, and the piano as well.

Umpak Bagus is a rather slow moving one, in which the acoustic guitar and doubled flute make for combination of percussiveness and melody over which we at times here some more rowdy electric guitar. Estrelas Do Amanhecer has a bit of Frippian guitar work in the back of acoustic tinkling and moody, whispered vocals. Quite nice.

With A 49a. Porta we move into Oldfield territory with up-beat guitars. When the string orchestra and flute set in we are square back in the Southamerican setting. The sharp solo electric guitar adds its two cents. A lot is going on here, maybe a bit too much, although the intense guitar solo is really quite nice. Good to have some more intensity too.

Carrossel continues this string or shortish tunes with folky acoustic guitar in the lead. At the end we get the same kind of long sustained notes on guitar as on the previous track. The Last Call is indeed the last one, and is in the Cuneiform style, and with good melodic material to boot. A worthy conclusion.

Conclusion

I am in two minds about this one, since there is no single type of music being stuck to. On the one hand we have the rather light, acoustic and flute oriented pieces which sounds very much like Southamerican prog from the seventies. On the other hand we have some ELPish oriented tunes with melodies that remind of tracks like Peter Gunn. By far the most liked by myself however are a number of more daring tracks in which the Cuneiform side of the band comes up: no flute, but the sax takes a more leading role, and there is plenty of room for tension in a chamber orchestra setting. I would have liked to hear more of that, although 77 minutes of it, would probably have me growing tired of that as well. In that sense, the variety of this album is likely to be a blessing. I do get the impression this is more a collection of tracks recorded over a longer period than an actual album.
