Summary

After their extremely, and deservedly, well-received debut double album, Revolutions it took the duo some time to record a new one. Now the band, its mastermind Robert Reed and siren Christina, are back with Seven, a single album about the seven deadly sins.

The music

The first of the seven deadly sins is Gluttony, which opens very much in a Yes style. On the debut, each of the four epics took on a certain style of a well-known prog band. This time, they have not taken these pains. I hear a bit of Genesis, quite a bit of Yes in this song. There are many vocal harmonies and second voices here, all sung by Christina. The guitar can be Oldfieldian at times. The music is what one might prototypical symphonic rock, with its many meandering passages and alternations. Among the female progvocalist Christina is in a class of her own. The song is long enough to harbour, in a sensible way, balladic episodes, which are in my opinion melodically the most interesting passages, and the speed up at the end is a good one too. The guitar solo following up is not so typically prog, more of a rock solo. The tail of the song also has some enticing melodies and a Peter Gabriel-like passage in vocoded fashion. The guitar sound here is more like Clapton's Layla, very high pitched.

Envy is a light piece of work, there are some links to Maggie Reilly's vocal track with Mike Oldfield, but the chorus melody is way better. In fact, the chorus melody is a majestic highpoint on this album, punctuated by piano. The middle part is very peaceful, featuring keys that remind of Child In Time. Then the music sets in again, bringing us back to symphonic rock. What a great track, although it can surprise fellow train travellers when the person sitting across from them has tears dropping from his eyes. (Good thing, I had my Fisherman's Friends with me.)

The Vienna Symphony Orchestra opens on Lust with real life string, although quite soon we are back at plain ol' prog. In fact, this song brings us in addition to the orchestra, music in the style of the first two songs, with added to this mix a jazzrock guitar solo and marimba to illustrate the versatility of the band. The finale is again a good one.

On Greed, the guitar sound owes quite a bit to Steve Howe. After some typical progrock runs (the usual off-beat rhythms), the vocal melody is again a good and memorable one. For some reason when I hear these vocals, I would think the title of this song is Pride. There is a certain upliftingness to the music that makes it come closest to Yes.

Anger is by far the shortest track on this album, under six minutes. It opens quietly with acoustic guitar, and notwithstanding its title, it stays quiet throughout. The song reminds me of Robert Plant's Big Log and a bit of the blues side of Gary Moore. This is mainly due to the guitar playing.

Pride brings the by now well-known ingredients: Christina's many vocals, the varied guitar work, and also plenty of organ and some prominent bass work. The link to Yes on this song is particularly strong, also melody wise at times.

Sloth is the closer, opening rather lamentlike, the guitar crying out in pain in the chorus. One of the better choruses too, the music is almost film like, and has a certain tendency for melodrama. All in good taste though. The middle part is slow piano, followed by the guitar setting in, in typical Floyd style.

Conclusion

Although the main gist of this album is pure symphonic rock as have come to know it, with main reference being Yes, Robert Reed has included some new elements as well: a real orchestra, a varied guitar sound, both the usual symphonic kind but also heavy, and jazzy. Notwithstanding that Reed does most of the work, Christina is the icing on the cake, rising to great heights with a vocal melody such as the one in Envy, my favourite of the album, and worth buying the album for by itself. The remainder is good, solid symphonic rock with a sprinkle of moments of brilliance.
