Summary

Well y'all know him of course.

The music

To cut it short: I've heard of a lot of Kitaro in the past and to be honest, although I sincerely like his music, I had never expected him to surprise me with yet another album of his hand. Although he changed his style somewhat in the late eighties with performances by Jon Anderson and a more "bandlike" approach, Kitaro is now back on his own, but at first glance, gone are the three or four intertwined sweetly flavoured melodies. To the fore are the sounds of the a Tibetan monk chating (so it seems), the jungle, the pan flute and you might have guessed it by now, Kitaro has trod upon the path of world music. So it seems at first. Opening with the 13 minute Yamadashi (quite long for Kitaro), consisting of two parts Tanne and Prayer, the opening is almost a soundsketch with some vague pointers to Kitaro's older work (in the sounds mostly). Then we get a very nice fluent guitarwork a la Shine On Your Crazy Diamond. Very emotive. The yells on this track during the second half are quite hair raising in intensity. And the heavy percussion must not be overlooked either. At the end the older Kitaro (the melodic side, including those long ascending notes so typical of his sound) is integrated with the more primitive drum/guitar. A great track. Misty is only four minutes long. Subtle with soft bells, strings and flute this is quite a romantic song, longing and sad. Would go quite well with a party of people walking through the misty marshlands. With the windy sounds of Misty we move right into the titletrack. Thunderous, slow drums, acoustic guitar again indicate that Kitaro has gone back to the roots, his musical roots, anyone's musical roots. After four minutes the music breaks into the melodic side of Kitaro again in a majestic and overpowering way. Great to listen to if you have marvelous equipment (I guess, since I do not own it). Wood Fairies is another relatively short track with some Floydish tendencies, but mostly just fairylike music, a bit meandering, but quite nice to relax with. The term New Age might pop up here, but I do not mean this is in any derisatory way. Satoboki is a mysterious track just short of 10 minutes in which the flute figures prominently again. This is lonely music with ghosts haunting the fields. Later on the music becomes more involved and percussive. In Kiotoshi the tortured vocals return and the music is flowing rhytmically, like waves crashing on the beach. Melodically, this is more like what we are used to from Kitaro.

Conclusion

A great album to my surprise, where a well-known musician takes a different route and succesfully. There's still enough of that typical Kitaro style music for everyone to be happy, but especially with regards to the atmosphere building, this is a highpiont in the mans career. Recommended.
