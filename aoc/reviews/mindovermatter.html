Galleon - Mind Over Matter

Artist: Galleon
Title: Mind Over Matter
Label: self produced
Length(s): 65 minutes
Year(s) of release: 1998
Month of review: 09/1998

Line up

Goeran Fors - bass, vocals, keyboards
Dan Fors - drums and percussion
Ulf Pettersson - keyboards
Mikael Vaern - guitars

Tracks

1) Art Gallery 14.14
2) Operating Manual 10.44
3) Starborn 8.02
4) House Of Plenty 12.30
5) The Wanderer 21.19

Summary

Certainly not their first album, but still trying to do everything by themselves. I never heard any of their work, so I am a tabula rasa where this band is concerned.

The music

Opening with the radio noise of Art Gallery, we come then to a melodic part reminding one of a band like Twin Age. The vocals are somewhat like those of Morten Harket, but less high and less distinctive. After some four minutes the music seems to fire up a little, turn from poppy into rocky, but then we get a dreamy and mellow part, reminding me in part of Egdon Heath. The chorus is contrasts starkly being quite aggressive and in the prog metal vein. The keyboards have a certain church like quality to them. The next break is that when the music almost entirely stops: a slow mesmerizing keyboard, a droming sound and the vocals are all we can here. The silence builds up to a climax, erupting into music yet again. The style and atmosphere is comparable to that of Aragon in the Crucifixion. The music is on the whole keyboard dominated and the melodies are manifold and all likable. The song ends with synthetic strings. Operating Manual is totally different. It is quite jumpy and quirky, yet opens with "mellotron". The lyrics are quite ironic, about devices and operating them, probably speaking from the experiences of the keyboard player. After a soaring part, the music becomes quiet and thoughtful. The following keyboard solo reminds me of Fruitcake, while the bass is very prominently drones. Starborn is with eight minutes the shortest track on this album. Main ingredients of this song are the synthetic bagpipes, the slow but audible rhythm, the almost merry keyboards softly in the back, the almost anthemic vocals and an atmosphere that reminds me of Simple Minds' Belfast Child, but not sad, more hopeful. House Of Plenty opens with a remarkable melody and is thoroughly in the old Genesis vein. While the drummer plods on, the hushed vocals remind of Fruitcake again. This is like the first track keyboard rich progressive with an eye open towards the seventies, but also their mind in the nineties Compared with the previous songs this is the best song so far, with a magnificent ending. The closer is the twenty+ The Wanderer. Quite a bunch of lyrics go into this song. The first three minutes are filled with the tingling of acoustic guitar and vocals. Then the song unfolds itself with varying parts: mellotron riddled passages, playful acoustics, clear reverberating piano-like passages, although the vocal melodies are not as striking as the ones on previous songs.

Conclusion

Lovers of Genesis should certainly be able to enjoy these Banks influenced compositions (One For The Vine), but the more modern inserts also remind of the moodiness of Fruitcake and the majesty of some of the songs of IQ. The music has a tendency to sound very familiar very quickly. It is hard to tell whether this is because the music is so derivative or so catchty, but a fact is that this is an album with some wonderful music on it (most notably House of Plenty). Unfortunately it is also sometimes a bit longwinded, like the closer The Wanderer. Still, I do not think the lovers of said Genesis-era will mind.
