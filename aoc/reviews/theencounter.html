Summary

Strings Arguments is one of those Japanese experimental noisy bands that have taken mid seventies crimsonesque jamming a couple of steps further, right into the devil's mouth. This is the band's first live album (as the title already might suggest). SA is also three members of Six North with KBB's violin player and guitarist Okamoto.

The music

The album starts off with Improvisation, which warms the listener to what's coming. It feels like the band are doing their musical stretching, slowly picking up speed and intensity to work to the track's climax. Where normally albums like this might initially wash over you, this one gives you the chance to get in it. Good stuff.

From Sri Lanka to Titan also starts more or less slowly, but as it comes alive it displays a lengthy section in which violin is pushed forward by bass, drums and guitar. This is followed by a more guitar dominated section. At the end the instruments share in a remarkably melodic finish, almost too melodic for a track like this. Although the solos aren't all that solo, this track does sound less instrumentally balanced and a bit more freaky than the opener.

Everything Becomes Circle starts sweetly with violin, taken over by guitar, not unlike Holdsworth, to be relieved by the violin again towards the end. It could use a bit more ecclectism, since in time it gets past the initial section, but it doesn't sound like that. Bit too diffident.

Silence, Darkness starts with a drum solo, toms, and then a bit of hi hat too. Not all that interesting. The guitar returns, a bit more neurotic than Holdsworth now. This takes off into something pretty swirling, with pushing bass and drums as well. The mayhem slowly dies down to move into a more quiet guitar solo. The violin sound towards the end is pretty nice and layered (with just one violin?), reminding me a bit of UK. The climax is pretty good too.

Kyoto No Natsu starts with warming up on a guitar, but settles in soon enough into a lengthy build up, relieved on the way over by the violin. Still, on the way over the track doesn't appear to hit more than a local maximum around six minutes, after which we move into preamble land again. I guess Kyoto summer afternoons are pretty sweet, although they might have a bit of a breeze towards the end.

Kundabutter's start is marked by a drum roll that sounds like it can't wait anymore. The initially gypsy like violin, later whipping up into a frenzy, gives a gentle feel. After this the guitar kicks in, just as frantic, later briefly melodic before veering off again. Spicy track, this, but still nice.

Conclusion

Albums like these can get very technical and pushy on one. Don't try playing this in the background, you might hear only notes. These notes, however, on closer inspection turn out to be structured pretty soundly compositionally, even displaying subtlety and finesse at moments. Still pretty tough stuff, but if you like your share of this kind of stuff, this is a nice one.
