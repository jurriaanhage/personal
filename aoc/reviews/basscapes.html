Summary

I had never heard of this guy, so you might not have done so either. Only thing I can connect him with is Attention Deficit, since he has played with Michael Manring. In Scott's own words this is Trance Ambience For Horizontal & Vertical Bass.

The music

Opener Khumbum is a warm, slow moving introduction to the work of Drengsen. Although I'm not that familiar with the bass in ALL its possible forms, it seems that most if not all of the music is played on bass. However, the background seems to have been done with keyboards. On the other hand it sounds so "formless" that it might as well be bass in some form or other. On the foreground there are two melodies playing, one quite high and a bit wailing and the other more monotonous and lower. The music has a definite Indian feel (i.e. Ravi Shankar, but more Western and therefore easier for us to appreciate. Well at least for me.). The music can be likened to Fripps work on the guitar, although I feel emotion plays a bigger role there, the guitar being a more versatile instrument and Fripp a more inspired player. This does not however mean that the music of Drengsen is boring. Notwithstanding the repetitive nature of the music, he is able to bring about a sense of relaxation that for instance New Age music always fails to bring to me. I won't try to become vague and talk of being one with nature, but this kind of dark, subdued and subtle music can easily carry you away (especially if you hve just reviewed two progmetal discs and listened to two others). The music is quite constant in its approach: a few bass lines intermingle in a harmonious, nonobtrusive and melodic way. Goofy Wisdom opens much darker and later on the basswork comes close to electric guitar with a more cutting sound. The music is in fact now quite close to psychedelic music. After Love That Brought Us, we come to the tranquilly opening Out Of Ancient Shadows, that harbours a melodic solo. After Heavens Castle we come to the longish Crystal Dimension, again a ponderous piece with the noise of the sea. Later it develops into a noisy piece with flashes of lightning. The closer is Jaimilas Camel Caravan, which not surprisingly has an Eastern feel to it.

Some comments about Basscapes II: In addition to the first Basscapes, I also got a CDRom with some newer tracks to be released as part two in the "series". The largest difference at first "glance" is that percussion is also present on the five tracks that feature on this CD (total length over 37 minutes). The bassplaying is less pronounced and the percussion is more prominent than the bassplaying, hence too prominent. I'm not too happy with this myself since it takes away the focus from the bassplaying. And, when focussing on the percussion it turns out that this is just not interesting enough. In track four, the music becomes very melodic instead of the more atmospheric earlier pieces. I'm not sure why Drengsen chose to include such clear percussion, but I prefer the old set-up. The percussion in the last piece is rather nice however with some very nice sounds, while the bass playing is rather loud and heavy here.

Conclusion

Notwithstanding the stylistic sameness to the compositions on this album, it harbours enough melody to captivate for the entire 69 minutes. Of course, one must be able to deal with the longish excursions on bass, but there's nothing trivial or trite about it. In view of some of the writings on the booklet I was thinking New Age at first, but contrary to many of those Indian guru's naming albums of the nineties and late eighties, the music on this album is not straightforward. Nor is it difficult or the bass-equivalent of a guitar hero record. It's a slow moving, ponderous musical statement, well adapted to playing late at night.
