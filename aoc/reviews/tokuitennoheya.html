Walrus - Tokuiten No Heya

Artist: Walrus
Title: Tokuiten No Heya
Label: ADDA-001
Length(s): 31 minutes
Year(s) of release: 2004
Month of review: [06/2006]

Line up

Shiba - vocals, flute
Goro - bass, backing vocals
Okabe - drums, backing vocals
Hideki - guitar, backing vocals

Tracks

1) A Moss Green Screen 6.17
2) Freedel & Cartel-leath 2.54
3) My Workshop 5.35
4) Deceiver 6.57
5) Interview 5.10
6) Quite Quiet Road 4.21

Summary

Walrus hails from Japan, and has a few albums out.

The music

The album starts out quiet enough with typically Genesis styled acoustic guitar and soft voiced vocals. Then we get some mellow mellotron as the song slowly unfolds, and the bass follows along nicely. Then we run into a piece of mean guitar, plodding along. But the vocals stay friendly (and Japanese). Slowly the music becomes rockier and rockier, and the guitar can be quite shrill and noisy. The closing section is acoustic again.

On the follow-up we rock out, with a relatively short and not so melodious track, which is closer to punk rock than anything else. The tempo is high, and the drive is strong. This kind of track makes it hard to pigeonhole the band. For My Workshop we return to the quiet, although in this case disquiet is more apt, music. The opening combines vocals and sinister sounding keyboards to something darkly atmospheric. The Japanese language helps here I think. The vocalist is not always on the note either, so it might be necessary to take that into account. Musically this is a strong track though, with some wailing guitar towards the end building a kind of wall of sound. Production wise things could be improved here, but the songs are interesting.

With [Deceiver] we are back to the alternative rock style of the second song. The song features mellotron (in case you find this important), but generally the song has that New Wave like character with a bit of the avant-garde and a tendency to quirky melodies. The second half has a lower pace, but more distortion in the guitar. Here the band shows their hand at building atmospheres, and the mellotron comes in again.

For Interview we return to the acoustic guitars and sensitive vocals, with an optimistic and restrained sound. Quite sixties this and the flute also comes in again, like on the opener. Very dreamy stuff with only a bit of e-guitar towards the end.

The closer opens with mellotron, with a strong hint of folkrock, very balladic, even though I do not know what he is singing about. But the vocal melody so strongly hints of folk, that the impression is there nonetheless. The melody is also very strong, indeed the best on the album. The guitar work at the end is strong, as is the song itself. It does sound very seventies.

Conclusion

Japanese bands have more and more come into their own. Take Walrus as an example, definitely inheriting parts of Genesis, Yes and King Crimson, but taking it into a direction of their own, including elements of New Wave and punkrock. You might need to get used to the vocals and the somewhat lo-fi approach, but the music is original enough, with some really nice parts and pieces. But I have to admit that prog needs a bit more polish than this for my tastes. It certainly takes some time getting used to.
