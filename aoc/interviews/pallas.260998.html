Pallas interview September 2nd 1998

Introduction

Interview with Pallas on the 26th of September 1998 in zaal Hedon in Zwolle, The Netherlands. At first there is Alan Reed, Niall and the new drummer, Colin Fraser. At the end Graeme Murray joins for a short while. Interviewers are Roberto Lambooy and Jurriaan Hage.

September 1997 we found ourselves in the catacombes of Zaal Hedon in Zwolle, The Netherlands. Pallas was about to make its longawaited way back to the Dutch stage after having left it in Spring 1993. The band has finally managed to get down to actually recording its third studio album since The Sentinel came out in 1984. After the more commercial The Wedge released in 1986, Beat The Drum, officially released in 1999 on Inside Out, seems to contain the best of both worlds and as yet by far there strongest and mature effort.

The interview

We might as well start where we left off at the first Pul Festival in 1993. There you said you would release an album that very year. What happened?

Alan: Well, basically a whole lot of unfortunate things got in the way. We were like we said ready and we had in fact started the recording at that point. We had written the songs and only a few things needed to be tied up, but we thought we had material for an album. But then things happened, particularly with our jobs and stuff. This meant that getting together proved to be getting very very difficult. It was always that one or another was doing something. Also, Mike (Stobbie) was getting increasingly busy with all his work especially since he was working towards getting a major deal of his own. So we kept on working towards the album, but we never seemed to get there. It became very frustrating and in the end, gradually we were losing interest as a consequence. Then, about a year ago I think Niall called me.

Niall: About a year ago, Graeme and I decided we wanted to make an album regardless of the siuation. We just felt it was necessary to do that. Alan came up to listen to some things we had and we wanted to run them past him and I think that at that time Alan was not convinced that the band would continue. But I think that after he had heard what we had done, he got off on it again.

Alan: The material had a distinctive sound to it. It was clear that this was the material that we wanted to do. Some things about the previous stuff we weren't sure about. We didn't have it clear in our minds what direction we would take. The band was pulling in different directions, the writing wasn't cohesive, it wasn't really Pallas, a lot of it. But this stuff was. And there was some stuff that we really wanted to use, so we had to work on that

Niall: Ghost, Man Of Principles and All Or Nothing.

Alan: And then we had a collection that actually worked together. There's lots of other songs that we recorded, some are partly finished. They just did not fit into the cohesive whole. Some may actually appear in future. To be honest there's really a lot of material that will never see the light of day, because they're not representative of Pallas. What we have done now, is what we really liked to do. At some point we just started doing the things we needed to do: I was coming up in the weekends by plane or going by train overnight to Aberdeen on Friday, record vocals for two days and then geting back before work on Monday. Trying to snatch things where we could. And then Derek had his problem and we decided that he could not continue. A lot has happened in the past year, it's incredible what we've done. We had always thought we'd like shelve the previous project and start again next time.

What about releasing the non-Pallas stuff as side project. That seems quite usual these days.

Niall: I don't think we would do that. We are now totally fixed on the direction of Pallas.

Alan: That is the important thing, that takes priority over everything. For instance, things were taking so long that I had started working on some solo material with a friend of mine. We have actually been writing and developing. That is now on hold and may never happen now, cause there is only so much time and this is the important thing.

Colin: From an outside point of view, though I have been a friend of of Niall and the guys for some time, the project was stalled and stalled and at some point it gathered this momentum and enthusiasm. And particularly the last three months when the whole thing was coming together very quickly. The situation with Derek was that obviously he himself wasn't too good and he had a few personal problems so he decided he had enough. At short notice I was asked to come in help with the album, but then it turned out that when he got well he didn't want to do it anymore and so I was asked to join the band and I did. From then it has been non-stop.

This more or less takes care of the falling through of the Day of Dreams gig. Now one thing though: when you did not turn up, you did have a tape ready. Was this already in the planning or was it specifically for Day of Dreams?

Alan: Specifically for Day of Dreams. Basically what happened was that I had taken off a week to come ot Aberdeen and rehearse for the gig. The day I left it became clear that Derek would not be able to play and it was a desperate situation for us. We really don't like to let people down. It was very embarassing and we did in fact look very much forward to it and we had already worked towards that gig a lot. So we had some discussion about what we could do, since I was up in Aberdeen anyway. We could of course have turned up doing an acoustic set of something like that, but we didn't really want to and we didn't think it would be practical or worthwhile. So we decided to, in some way, say sorry and since we had already made some progress on some tracks and Beat The Drum was our current favourite at the time we thought we'd use the time to get some tracks as finished as possible. Also, we could then let people know that we were really working on something, that we were progressing, that there really would be an album, that we would be playing there, even though only at a later date. So we worked like maniacs for the rest of the week to break down on Beat The Drum especially and some other things. In the end it was quite a positive thing since it broke the back of the album and it started to get some reality. You could now outline the shape. In the end it probably did a lot of good, although it was a shame we couldn't come.

Later on the tape could also be had on request as a sort of album sampler. So that was a kind of bonus to come with it.

Alan: Yes. Basically, we made enough to give one to everyone if the place was sold out and so we made some 500 tapes. So, as it turned out the message that we were not coming got out to a lot of people and a lot of people didn't come, so there were quite a lot of the tapes left. Then we thought, why don't we just give them out over the internet and if people wanted one they could get it? And it turned to be very popular and we ran out of them.

One other disc of Pallas is Arrive Alive that just came out. From your history I glanced that 10000 copies were sold when it is acutally quite rare down here.

Alan: Well it was only released in the UK. It was before the band was signed.

Niall: The thing is that the thing only existed on vinyl and in this day and age eveything has to be out on CD. And also because we went on internet, we started getting so many requesrs asking us when we were going to release it on CD. So it seemed a very logical move to do it, although some people were quite surprised by it.

I also read something about the Pallas EP. Is this something for a next rareties disc?

Alan: Well, that are some bits and pieces that are in fact available. There is some kind of EP that we put out with Nightmares. And there was some live stuff as well.

The Southampton demo you mean?

Alan: No, no. I mean the live tracks that we used as B-sides for the singles on The Wedge. The Southampton tape was recorded during the last concert of the Wedge tour. We didn't record it for release purposes.

Niall: But then in the end people were asking for it, so we did it fanclub only. Sketches was just for loyal fans, to show what we were doing. Sketches keeps coming up again and again and people are treating it like an album or so.

Alan: Sketches was recorded with very basic equipment. How we worked then, is that we basically recorded everything during rehearsed. As something coalesced, we tried to get a version onto tape.

There were different versions of Arrive Alive on tape and vinyl containing different songs. Are they all on this CD?

Alan: Yes they are so it contains more stuff than any of them. And it also contains the Paris Is Burning EP.

Writers note: it does not feature the song Heart Attack.

Okay, about the album now. Last time, 1993, you told us that it would be a mix of the Sentinel and The Wedge.

Niall: Which is exactly what it is.

Alan: It is true actually. It has got elements of the Sentinel definitely. In a way perhaps The Wedge didn't have enough of it in it. That was actually a deliberate move on our part at the time. To try to be different, not only from what we did, but from what everybody else was doing. I think Beat The Drum has elements of everything we have done and as such has caught the essence of the band.

Niall: That I think was what attracted Alan to it. Because we got that balance, the way we always wanted to be, in a way. Also to try to retain, I hope it doesn't cliche', to capture the live aspect of the band, you know? Because people always used to say "Oh, you're so powerful live" and we didn't quite seem to catch that in the studio. So it is quite raw; in places it can be quite raw.

For me the shorter tracks like Man Of Principles remind me of The Wedge, being more catchy, while the longer more involved tracks automatically remind me of The Sentinel.

Niall: Well there were those tracks that we really wanted to do like

Alan: Yeah, the version on Sketches were not the finished article.

I couldn't quite catch from my copy of the booklet who does what in this band.

Alan: Well it varies with every song. Most songs are a group effort, although someone generally has the basic idea. And then we go through a process with the band and in the end it is quite difficult to tell who is responsible for what.

Colin: Like on Call To Arms, the opening track of the album. I had gotten together with Niall and Graeme for the first time at Graeme's house playing an electronic drumkit. During a jam we came up with this little section that we actually placed into Call To Arms. And this is typically what, I think, Pallas works like.

Alan: There are a few songs that are more less personal efforts such as Blood And Roses.

And the lyrics?

Alan: Well they are mostly mine, but Graeme also does much on the lyrics. Some of them like Blood And Roses I did not change much at all.

The first two tracks have this strong theme of mobilization in them.

Alan: Well, it is. And it is as much addressed to ourselves, that we would get our things together. Lyrically we did not really go for any theme, but it does seem that in the end the lyrics sort of fit together. Sometime a lyrics start out to be about some thing and in the end it turns out to be about something else altogether. Sometimes I try to write a song about something, but it never works out that way. I really don't know what half the songs are about anymore.

Graeme Murray joins for a short while.

On my copy of the booklet there were no titles with the lyrics, and so when I went from Hide And Seek to Insomniac I didn't even notice it.

Graeme: Well, they are very much related since they are about the same kind of madness.

Alan: I got this idea since for some time I was working a lot over the internet and talking to people on the other side of the world during the night. I also started to use the internet to get new ideas for stories. It was very strange that I was talking to people in the middle of the night while for them it was the middle of the day. Sometimes I called people up in Australia, telling them "Hey, this is the BBC" and they would go "Why are you calling us now? It must be the middle of the night over there?".

Graeme: That, and combined with the kids that spend their time in bed on the internet. This ultimate internet surfer being absorbed into his PC.

To close down, what do you think is the strongest point of Pallas?

Graeme: Well, I think that one the strengths of Pallas is that we write songs and not just musical pieces. A lot of the bands in progressive rock in these modern days, they can write complicated stuff and play it really well, but if you take it away, there is no song there.

Alan: We start with the song, but just play it in the format of progressive rock.
