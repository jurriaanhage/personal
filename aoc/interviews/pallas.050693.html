Pallas interview June 5th 1993

Introduction

Interview of Pallas in the persons of Niall Matthewson and Graeme Murray by Jurriaan Hage and Roberto Lambooy at the first Uden festival on June 5th 1993. Broad transcription was made by Roberto Lambooy.

Past and Future

We'll start off in the first year, back in '78.

Graeme: A long time ago

Eventually you made a deal with Harvest and recorded the Sentinel.

Graeme: That's right

The sound of the album was epical (birth of a new term).

Niall: Yes, yes, good word

I made it up myself I believe

Graeme: In retrospect we felt that the Sentinel was a good way to start with EMI. We tried to do a sort of a concept album, and really for a first album that was sort of stupid. But we did it.

Niall: We did what we did. People did like it, an awful lot of people liked it. Am I ravaging? No! It's a classic.

Graeme: We think now that the CD is made and you compare the sound of CD to the sound to the vinyl album...

Niall: ...that would be the only regret; the fact that we didn't actually mix it. That was the greatest thing, cause when we got the master tapes back after we got back from touring it was a totally different thing! You know it wasn't the way we liked it, the way we wanted it.

Graeme: We tried to record a modern sounding album, and while we did it sounded very modern. But when we heard the mix, it sounded very dated. So it was a bit of a shock.

Niall: For the CD we haven't been able to remix it, but we have been able to remaster it and bring out certain aspects. We are a lot happier with it

Also now that all the songs are in it, in it?

Niall: Yeah.

Graeme: Yeah, that as well

Niall: There's no point getting to the technichalities. we had certain ideas of what we were going to do when we were there, you know The order of the songs we were doing. Then some of them got rejected and some of them more commercial songs put in their places. I mean that's fine, it's not the end

Graeme: It's the way we want it now, but it wasn't then.

Would you have preferred to start off with the Wedge?

Graeme: Well, we weren't an awful lot from the time we recorded the Sentinel album till the time we recorded the Wedge we worried a huge amount about how to record, a huge amount about modern sounds. In fact it was a time when there was an awful lot happening, technology wise and there were a lot of new keyboard sounds, and everything. When we did The Sentinel album we didn't have the resources available to us to get a lot of that gear. I mean we had a synclavier, but we had it for two days, you know.

Niall: It's something internal to us you go through these processes of recording something and then obviously you're so close to it that you tend to reject it, you know, that's the natural thing. You get so close to something that you've had enough of it. It's only in retrospect that you can look at the thing and say: "That was it, that was the way we wanted it at the time, that's what we believed in". We went on and we did The Wedge, which is a much more up to date album, because, you know, that's what we thought we wanted to do at the time. The two things are valid. But now we've come to the point where we can see that's an aspect of us and that's an aspect of us: The Sentinel and The Wedge. Now we're in the place where we can combine it and get ourselves together

Graeme: The new stuff's inbetween, actually. It's a blend of the sort of modern techniques of the Wedge with the big keyboard sound. See when we signed for EMI there was an awful lot of pressure on us to produce great singles. To get airplay on the radio. And now we don't give a fuck we're not signed to a major label anymore.

Majors and Video Games

That was one of my questions about major labels, because if you look at the bands that came up when you did like IQ, Marillion, Twelfth Night, Pendragon and yourself you see that only Marillion made it. And three of those bands, the bands that are still alive, have their own label.

Graeme: The problem is there is not one single person in a position of importance in any major record company anywhere who understands this music. They just don't understand it.

Niall: Well, I think it's not that they don't understand it. It's like a package of soappowder. They can't sell it they can't see it's a sellable thing. I remember when I went to school. I used to go to school with my Yes albums under my sleeve. There was a whole different thing there all together. Another ambience.

Times have changed, too much, for this kind of music?

Graeme: Times have changed. Kids used to go for music in general, kids used to go to school with stacks of albums under their arms: "Hey, check this new band". Now they go to school with their Nintendo Gameboy in their pocket and half a dozen games in it and say "Hey, check this new game". That's what their into. Music is not, it doesn't hold the importance that it used to, not in our modern culture. Good heavens, what a heavy interview.

If you look at a band like Nirvana you know, I believe that they risen, because people who liked punk in the old days have better jobs, they have more power to do something with those bands.

Graeme: Nirvana came along and offered us a raw power. This is rock 'n' roll again.

How long was it then that you played your last gig, at all?

Graeme: June 1988 in the Marquee was the last gig we played. So we were effictively in forced retirement for a while

But you did release Sketches in the while...

Graeme: Yeah, well you see what happened was: there were working ideas for new songs and we got to the point where we just had to stop, to go get the jobs and things, because our lives were falling apart. We were short of money.

So you're still an amateurband now

Graeme: Yes!

Money and Fame

You have jobs?

Graeme: We're working at the moment now. We have jobs yeah.

Just like IQ had to. They were a professional band and then they had to get jobs.

Graeme: That's right.

That's hard, hard work. But do you still mean, mean to make music?

Graeme: We're making a new album. It's difficult to get it together, but we're doing it and I sincerely hope it's out for the end of this year.

On Centaur discs?

Graeme: On Centaur discs. That is a label that has been set up by CD Services in Dundee in Scotland. They have a company that specializes in symphonic music and synthesizer music and they sell compact discs. They wanted to have their own label and start putting out acts and when we got our stuff back from EMI, they said: well look, well this would be an ideal chance for us to watch our new company work. So I don't think we have any aspirations of signing another major deal. We're just quite happy to make our own albums in our own time, the way we want to do it; put them out and play live before our own audience.

But originally you had the idea that you would become famous.

Graeme: Of course!

That's what you all did it for?

Graeme: No, no, I think we didn't do it to be famous

Niall: We wanted to make lots of money.

Graeme: We did it we did, because we really enjoyed it. We really enjoyed writing our own music. Because, when you write your own music you write it because that's what you want to hear. So it's a bit as opposed to regular music. As opposed to listening to someone else's. We wanted to play in a band, write our own music and be able to keep on playing in a band and writing our own music. And the only way to do that is to be succesful.

Would you say that being signed to a major company takes a way some of the pleasure of making music, you can't play what you want to play?

Graeme: Yes! A: That's what being signed up the a major company is. It's very very much like you are completely in their arms.

Graeme: They own you. A: Yeah, they do own you.

Graeme: They own your life I tell ya this is your life if they want to they just go (trashes a piece of paper)... And that is it, its simple. They just crush... they effectively end your life.

Niall: It's big business, isn't it. Once again to come back to the selling of commodities, the record company says: "There's a package, sell that".

They're run by managers, not by musicians.

Graeme: Yes, they're ruled by accountants, they're ruled by businessmen. A: It's just like every other business, run by large intermediates. You need access to their distribution network.

Graeme: You get a guy working for Nesquick selling chocolatebars being made managing director of EMI records. One week they're selling chocolate, the next week they're selling music. And these guys come to their boss: "Hello, boss. This is a great new band listen to this. It's great isn't it? And they say yeah yeah it's great we wanna get behind this. okay yeah do it". It's just its so destroying. We had a bad experience but then we came back. So did Fish, so did Marillion...

Graeme: So what's happening is that the major record companies don't really have any interest in this kind of music. Yet who is the biggest grocing band in the world last year?

Genesis?

Graeme: Last year? Pink Floyd. Not Madonna, not Michael Jackson, not not the Beegees, not never. Yet are record companies don't seem to take it on board.

Genesis and Pink Floyd and Yes were just right on time.

Graeme: Yeah.

In the beginning. Then Genesis commercialized a lot and that brought, well, real fame.

Niall: Well pink Floyd did well.

They made it, but they were early.

Niall: You can't really use that analogy cause they came up in a different era all together.

Yes that's what I mean, it's bad timing.

Graeme: What happened was that in the early eighties there was this progressive rock revival, as it was called in the British press. And the press tried to hype it it. If this concert had happened ten years ago when that was all starting to break, I think it might have actually taken off. We would have been far more succesful and a lot of bands who didnt have the amount of succes Marillion did, they really would have been far more succesful. If there were events like this around Europe and around Britain, but the trouble was: there were to many people who didn't want to see it happen, because, in Britian anyway, Yes, Genesis, Emerson, Lake & Palmer, all the early prog bands, used to get slayed, even when they were succesful the press tore them apart. They just hated them.

They thought they were to pretentious?

Graeme: Yes

Niall: What does that mean?

You say it sounds good, so that's it. They talk about pretence, they analyse it.

Graeme: Yeah, yeah. Exactly. They don't just listen and say that sounds good. Listen to these lyrics, what are they trying to tell us. If you want to get into lyrics, fine but it's not where it ll ends. If it sounds good then go for it.

So what are you gonna do next? You're gonna record your new album?

Graeme: There's a couple of more shows to do, then we're gonna start getting this album done, aren't we.

Niall: Yes, dad,

Graeme: We are, we really are. And it'll be a cracker.

So we have to expect something between The Sentinel and The Wedge?

Graeme: Yeah.

The compactness and the heavyness of The Wedge.

Graeme: And a bit of romanticism.

Niall: I suppose in a lot of ways The Sentinel was a modern album. The Sentinel>> was definitely a side of us and The Wedge was another side of us.

The albums are very different.

Graeme: Yeah, of course. We had the change with Alan as well. I mean Alan is a better singer.

Graeme: The band was going in a cetrain direction with Huan. Sort of towards something quite bizarre on the visual side.

Niall: Yeah.

Graeme: And Niall is sort of a rock 'n' roll man a bit of the old... We were trying to something back then, sort of, didn't have the dosh to do, the money to do. We tried to put on a huge stage show, like Floyd or somebody would put on.

Niall: I think probably that was one of the reasons. What happened was in the beginning we felt like quite much. We had a big PA that we had built ourselves, all from scratch, and cross tubes that we had built ourselves. We used to go on tour trying to put up a show.

Graeme: And so we got such a reputation.

Niall: There was no other band that was doing it you know. We started to get a lot of interest from shams, you know all of the magazines and the papers. It was because of this, that we worked so hard to begin to try and form a show.

Graeme: I don't think we imagined, we'd get signed a lot of worries.

Niall: You see what's happening in the popcharts, and we thought let's just put on our show and see what happens. And then we got signed. We got on the show and sounds came in, we became involved. We got displayed up on the frontpage. Then we got management to put us up. Then we got a date at the Marquee in London, then we got Reading and then we got to do our album. You know, it all went like that.

Niall: Well I don't think we could have done it, if we hadn't had that initial artwork building the show together.

So it was a bit like a gimmick.

Graeme: A gimmick? No, no.

But you did have an explicit image, something that made you different from other bands. Something that made you marketable.

Graeme: I think.. there are certain keywords to success. What I think one is Originitality, another must be buzz, people talking about you, you know. I mean that's a couple of very important keywords for getting anywhere. You just gotta be a bit different.

And the later we get, the more....I mean: how can you be original these days. It's so difficult

Graeme: You can't. You just do your own thing, mate. If people like it, fine. If they don't, fine.

It's also personality, charisma. It takes people, like Fish, he grabs people.

Graeme: You see the kind of music we play is very difficult to play it and jump around a lot.

Niall: To set the stage up to run back very quickly, you know.

But I think Alan does react well to musical parts. For a lot of bands with with a couple of minutes with no singing the singer just stands there with his hands in his pockets and you don't know what to do. But Alan seems to be reacting pretty well to that.

Graeme: I remember seeing Hawkwind back in 1972 and all they had for lights were two strokes on the audience all night. And I went home with the worst fucking headache that I've ever had in my life from a gig. I mean there were just all these silhouettes sort of.

We saw it last month and they had stroboscopic light again. GM : you sad man. You sad sad man. Welcome to home. So is that alright.

Well I ask you some other questions. What about the live tape, are you gonna, gonna do it on CD?

Graeme: I would have thought that would be unlikely. A: I don't think we have a master.

Graeme: I think we'd rather do a new one.

A new one.

Graeme: Yeah, now that we're playing again, we'll probably do a new one.

Sketches

And Sketches?

Graeme: Well Sketches is a funny little thing. That's one knocked togother out of various takes and bits and pieces.

I still think Sketches sounds good.

Graeme: It's just a demo.

Niall: It was never meant to be anything other than that.

Maybe it's the purity of the tracks then... What's strange about Skecthes is that there are some instrumental tracks on it. A bit like the sound of what the modern Tangerine Dream makes.

Niall: Sure, they're just ideas, they're just not goods. That's why it's called Sketches.

They're just a rough ideas, and you recorded them, and think: well maybe later we'll do something with them

Niall: But of course the band was resting.

Graeme: Put it together. That was my idea behind it.

So you wanted to show more or less that even though you weren't playing on stage, you were still a band?

Niall: We've done all these bits and pieces you know, so let people hear it.

Graeme: I think what is significant, is that our line up has been pretty stable. Because I mean, we all get home. Yes we do.

Niall: I don't think we do.

Graeme: We all get on pretty well, yes we do. A: If it wasn't for the money I would...

Would you say that now that you have something else to do, working, its easier to keep the band together in the sense that you aren't together the whole day, so you don't get fed up with each other.

Graeme: It's easier to keep the band together, because we can pay our mortgage, we can put food in our fridge, we don't have cold summons coming in for the payment of bills. I mean we had to stop because we were financially destroyed.

Niall: I think we were emotionally destroyed, emotionally destroyed as well. Because there's the financial aspect, we would have ridden that like any other storm. It's the emotional thing that got us.

Graeme: We tried. We put our heart and soul, we put everything we humanly could to make a success of the band and it still wasn't enough. Because, I mean, we kept, it was a running joke, we kept in mind: things can't get any worse. And five minutes later the truck with all the gear would crash. It was unbelievable, you know. And as Niall says, we were all emotional cripples.

A lot of success in the future..: maybe we'll see you again.
